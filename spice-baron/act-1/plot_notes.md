# Act 1 — Plot Notes

## Scene 1: The Reckoning (Execution)

- The Baron's term for public execution is **the Reckoning** — formal, impersonal, which makes it worse; the word implies debt settled, order restored
- The previous chef's offense: stealing a small amount of spice to bring home to a woman in the concubine — a secret relationship, tenderness expressed as contraband
- The woman is made to watch; she is positioned in the crowd specifically so the chef can see her watching
- **The rule**: looking away is taboo. If a guard notices you averting your eyes, you are brought forward and made to stand closer. This is part of the punishment's design.
- Gardner knows this rule without being told — he has absorbed the cruel grammar of this life; he keeps his eyes forward and still
- This demonstrates, early, the distinction between Gardner and newer or more defiant souls: he endures by knowing exactly where the lines are
- The execution is a variant of the Blood Eagle — ritualized, deliberate, public spectacle; the prose should be ceremonial and tight, not frantic
- Establishes: the Baron's philosophy in action (the chef's love was a weakness exploited), Gardner's learned endurance, and the cost of feeling anything in this place

## Scene 2: The Greenhouse Escape

- Gardner flees to the vanilla and coffee section of the greenhouse complex
- The vanilla is in bloom — fragrant, fleeting; this detail should be specific and sensory
- He is among the cobots; their quiet industry is a counterpoint to the violence he just witnessed
- The Baron follows him into the greenhouse
- **The Baron's speech**: power, ownership, having. "Having is the most important thing." He is loud, sweeping, commanding — a philosopher of cruelty performing his thesis; civilized in register, which makes it worse
- **The Baron plucks an orchid bloom, crushes its fleshy petals, and drops it to the ground** — not rage, not drama; casual, almost absent-minded. The act is the point.
- Baron informs Gardner of the new chef

## Scene 3: The New Chef Arrives

- Arrival coincides with cinnamon peeling — sensory, labor-rich scene; red-brown bark curling in shallow baskets, the smell of it close and sweet
- The Baron introduces Táiwò to the assembled staff or workers; he is visibly excited — this Chef is a prestigious figure from Yoruba, a prize
- Táiwò has not yet been bent or broken by the Plantation; he speaks to Gardner like a person — not performing kindness, just offering it naturally; Gardner registers this as extraordinary
- This is their first exchange; the Baron's presence makes it formal and observed, but something passes between them regardless

## Act 3 — Gardner in Yoruba (Notes Seeded from Act 1 Development)

- Gardner travels to Yoruba with Táiwò and/or as part of the emissary arrangement
- First stop: **Oshun**, the cultural capital — the river city; its communal, tended beauty rhymes with Gardner's own nature and with the greenhouse
- He is brought into Kehinde's plan: to take over and liberate the Plantation, and secure control of the spice supply for Yoruba
- Kehinde reveals herself as Táiwò's twin — or Gardner pieces it together; the Táiwò/Kehinde structure (scout sent ahead, general follows) becomes legible
- Gardner gives testimony — his knowledge of the Plantation's layout, defenses, cobots, supply lines, the Baron's habits — making the operation possible
- The question of whether Táiwò's love and Gardner's recruitment are separable is left unresolved; it lives in the room

## Epilogue (Notes Seeded from Act 1 Development)

- Kehinde's party arrives to find the Baron's spent corpse strung up and Gardner draped across the chair
- Gardner's offer is a trade, not a surrender: Yoruba gets the spice, the trade, the access — but the Plantation belongs to him and to the people in it, and they will be treated as people
- He is taking ownership not as an authoritarian but as someone who belongs there; he is the caretaker, not the Baron's replacement
- This echoes August's original vision, but Gardner does it with his eyes open
- Kehinde may push back before accepting — she is a general negotiating terms, not receiving tribute
- The Girls are now his guards, dressed in armor and tattered gowns; the image completes the thread opened at the first Reckoning
- The leaving/staying tension with Táiwò is never resolved — Gardner remains, which was always the only answer he had; the grief of that is part of what he carries into the future

## Act 4 — The Blooming (Notes)

- Gardner "blooms" the Baron: a methodical, deliberate act rendered entirely in botanical language
- The Baron is made to watch via a mirror placed in front of him — he is forced to witness his own reduction, as Gardner and others were forced to witness the Reckoning
- The passage is prolonged and lyrical; suggestive rather than explicit; each stage described as a gardening act
- Register: *peeling, plying, separating, exposing* — the Baron as specimen, not subject
- **Gardner's inner state**: not anger — anger would make him too much like the Baron; instead he is precise, present, almost tender; the way he is with the plants; the mushrooms have cracked him open and he is somewhere transcendental
- **The gall metaphor**: in his altered state, Gardner sees it — the Baron was always a gall; a growth that forms around a wound, the plant's own tissue proliferating abnormally around something it could never expel; all that flamboyance, all those rooms full of things, all that noise — and at the center, a hollow; a tiny pit around some old wound, hardened over, protected by everything the Baron became; the gall doesn't know it's a gall
- Gardner doesn't forgive him; he understands him the way a gardener understands a diseased plant; the cruelty wasn't strength, it was a growth pattern; and that understanding is what releases the hatred
- **The DeGall name**: has been doing this work all along — *de gall*, of the gall — without the reader knowing it until this moment; the name is the novel's hidden key
